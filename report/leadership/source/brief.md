# Federal ICR Items 12-15 Benchmark

*Findings and Recommendations for TSA Economic Analysis*

September 9, 2026

**Executive summary**

A review of 73 recent federal Information Collection Requests (ICRs), including ten TSA collections, supports five findings for Economic Analysis Branch leadership.[@TECH|Executive summary; sections 2, 9-10]

**1. Strong federal practice is distributed.** No single reviewed agency supplies the complete model. Useful methods combine explicit burden activities, supported labor valuation, clear cost boundaries, and quantified explanations of change.

**2. TSA has meaningful strengths, particularly in Item 12.** Reviewed collections distinguish activities, applicant and employer effort, transition cohorts, and occupations. Several also expose Federal workload equations. Preserve these foundations.

**3. Published TSA statements show consistency and reconstruction risks.** Several statements contain conflicting subtotals, Federal hours, table and narrative costs, or wage footnotes. These differences can impede an analyst's ability to reproduce and reuse the intended estimate. They are analytical control issues, not merely formatting problems, that stronger links between the model and its outputs could prevent.

**4. The principal opportunity is a common quantitative record.** Each material input should be tied to its source, unit, period, and transformation. This source-bound model would produce Excel and Items 12-15 outputs after validation: one analytical source of truth for every representation. Human review remains responsible for assumptions and cost scope.

**5. Federal examples provide concrete methods to adopt.** Record which collection owns each obligation to avoid double counting; include preparation in measured burden; separate purchases from internal effort; and quantify Item 15 changes from an identified prior estimate.

**Recommended next step.** Consider common QA controls and a bounded prototype on one upcoming collection, followed by comparison of assumptions across similarly shaped TSA collections.

This review identifies practical controls for future economic analysis; it does not evaluate individual analysts or infer undocumented production causes. Detailed methodology and supporting evidence are retained separately.

# 1. Why this matters

Items 12-15 describe connected parts of an estimate: respondent time and labor valuation, non-hour respondent costs, Federal costs, and changes from the prior estimate. A reader should be able to move between these parts without encountering different populations, incompatible periods, or conflicting totals.[@SRC-0001|Items 12-15, pp. 2-3]

For TSA, the value extends beyond preparing a Supporting Statement. Burden and cost inputs may be reused in economic, policy, program, and regulatory analysis. When the published estimate cannot be reconstructed, the next analyst must resolve ambiguities before deciding whether the estimate is suitable for reuse. That work competes with substantive analysis for scarce branch capacity.

Repeatable methods can reduce that reconstruction burden. A renewal should reveal which requirements, populations, times, wages, or prices changed and how those changes affect the estimate. It should not require rediscovering the relationship between a narrative number and a separately maintained table.

## What the evidence can tell leadership

The benchmark reviewed **73 unique recent controls across 38 agency/component labels**, with TSA as the focal application. Its principal quality lens was independent reconstruction: can a reader follow disclosed inputs and assumptions to reported outputs? The review examined public statements and supporting materials and replayed selected calculations.[@TECH|Sections 1-3; Appendix E]

This was a purposive search for useful methods and counterexamples, not a representative sample. Scoring was performed by one analyst. The results support learning from particular methods and identifying documented inconsistencies; they do not estimate an agency-wide error rate or government-wide ranking.

**Confidence is strongest where the evidence is directly observable:** displayed components and totals, quoted rates, footnote transformations, and described activity structures. Confidence is more limited where the intended scope, internal precision, administrative inputs, or production history are unavailable. Some reviewed packages were pending, so public availability does not imply final approval.

The branch can act on this evidence without resolving every uncertainty. Arithmetic and representation checks are appropriate even when the intended replacement value remains unknown. Substantive questions about populations, timing, and cost boundaries should remain visible for analyst resolution.

## A practical federal frontier

The table identifies transferable methods that improve reconstruction. Each example teaches a bounded practice; it does not validate the collection's entire estimate.

| Area | Strong practice | Useful example and TSA application |
| Burden structure | Distinguish activities and obligations already counted elsewhere. | BLS occupational injury survey: distinguish existing and newly induced recordkeeping.[@SRC-0008|Item 12, recording burden and Table 9] |

| Item 13 | Separate work done internally from purchased services. | OSHA extinguishers: quantities and make-or-buy structure prevent double charging.[@SRC-0060|Items 12-13] |

# 2. What the benchmark found

## Three methods worth transferring first

**Assign obligations before adding hours.** The BLS example distinguishes establishments already required to keep OSHA records from those newly required to keep records for the survey. For TSA, an obligation ledger - a record of which collection owns each requirement - should identify the responsible control and explain exclusions or transfers. This prevents shared requirements from being counted twice.[@SRC-0008|Item 12, recording burden and Table 9]

**Measure the whole task.** Census's Annual Integrated Economic Survey testing distinguishes preparation, questionnaire-reported estimates, and paradata. These observations differ in scope and statistic. Time spent in an instrument does not necessarily include preparation, coordination, or record retrieval; a median cannot silently stand in for a mean. TSA timing evidence should identify the activity measured, respondent segment, observation period, and statistic before it is used to validate a burden assumption.[@SRC-0099|Printed p. 19, burden measurement comparison]

**Make Item 15 a numerical explanation.** The Coast Guard security-plan example removes 980 two-minute addendum responses, or approximately 33 annual hours. This is useful because the change in a requirement is linked to an interpretable workload effect. Its underlying administrative query is unavailable, so the example teaches reconciliation rather than complete replication of every input.[@SRC-0174|Items 12 and 15, removal of CG-6025A]

For TSA, identify the prior estimate used as the baseline and quantify the changes leading to the new estimate. These Item 15 bridges should separately explain changes in responses, hours, and nonlabor dollars. A change in population, task time, collection requirements, or estimation method should have an identifiable effect. When multiple inputs change together, disclose how their joint effect is allocated rather than silently switching the comparison basis.[@TECH|Sections 8, 10 and 14]

## Transfer methods; revalidate assumptions

Do not import another collection's time, wage, price, or allocation share merely because its method is transparent. OSHA's purchased-service structure, for example, is useful even when consultation age and cost scope require fresh review for a TSA application.

These practices improve different parts of the same estimate: the obligation ledger defines what is counted, measurement supports the amount of work, and the change bridge explains why the result moved. They are complementary controls, not alternative templates.

# 3. What the benchmark shows about TSA

## Preserve the strongest analytical structures

Reviewed TSA statements often contain operational detail that a generic burden template would miss. Three examples illustrate what should become reusable branch practice.

**Aircraft Operator Security distinguishes tasks and Federal workloads.** Its respondent model separates 18 activities, while Federal costing identifies three workloads. That structure makes it possible to change a task frequency or review-time assumption without treating the entire collection as one undifferentiated estimate.[@SRC-0038|Items 12 and 14]

**Airport Security distinguishes who performs the work.** Applicant time is separated from airport data-entry effort. Keeping those roles distinct supports occupation selection, prevents a single labor rate from obscuring different tasks, and clarifies when activities are additive even though they concern the same applicant.[@SRC-0176|Item 12, STA applicant and airport data-entry activities]

**Flight Training Security models a transition rather than a flat population.** Year-specific candidate and provider cohorts reflect movement to a multiyear security threat assessment. Its wage discussion also explains a career-stage choice and identifies wage and benefits sources. These are substantive modeling choices that a common structure should preserve.[@SRC-0036|Item 12, cohort tables and wage-selection footnotes]

Flight training received 52 of 60 points on the study's Item 12 composite, close to the highest observed 53. This supports the assessment of its reviewed method, not an agency ranking.[@TECH|Sections 3, 5 and 9]

## Standardize the connections, not just the template

A useful common approach would retain these distinctions and link them consistently: the activity to its population; the task to its occupation and rate; the cohort to the correct year; and the Federal workload to the appropriate resource cost.

The examples on the next two pages show why those connections matter. Detailed analytical structure can coexist with conflicting published representations. Preserving the structure while strengthening reconciliation is more useful than replacing it with a simpler, less informative narrative.

# 3. What the benchmark shows about TSA

## Published values do not always reconcile

### Certified Cargo Screening Program: subtotal discrepancy

**Published issue.** In Item 12, Table 7, the displayed component hours sum to **6,049**, while the published subtotal is **4,451 hours**. The difference is **1,598 hours**.[@SRC-0040|Item 12, Table 7]

**Classification.** This is a confirmed discrepancy between displayed components and a displayed subtotal. The source does not establish whether a component, the subtotal, or an underlying version represents the intended estimate. The recomputed sum should not be treated as a certified replacement package total.

**Why it matters.** A downstream analyst cannot use both the component model and the subtotal consistently. Reuse requires resolving which values are intended, even though the table appears to supply the necessary inputs.

**Direct control.** Calculate subtotals from the same underlying activity values used to produce the table. Reconcile displayed totals under a documented rounding rule, and require a disposition when the values do not agree.

### Aircraft Operator Security: table/narrative conflict

**Published issue.** The first-flight checklist narrative reports annual labor cost of **$54,658**, while Table 2 reports **$20,165,327** for that activity. The smaller value also appears for the adjacent foreign-airport employee activity.[@SRC-0038|Item 12, first-flight checklist, foreign-airport employees and Table 2]

**Classification.** This is a displayed-value conflict. The repeated amount does not establish the internal editing history. The table and a recomputation from displayed rates are close but not identical, so the benchmark does not certify a new exact replacement cost.

**Why it matters.** A reader who extracts the narrative rather than the table obtains a materially different estimate for the same activity. A general review of whether the model “looks reasonable” is insufficient to reconcile those representations.

**Direct control.** Bind the narrative amount and table cell to the same validated result. After any revision, regenerate both and check that the final exported statement agrees with the workbook.

# 3. What the benchmark shows about TSA

## Rates and workload require separate checks

### Airport Security: applied wage and footnote conflict

**Published issue.** Applicant calculations use **$34.48 per hour**, while the wage-footnote transformation produces approximately **$50.19 per hour**. The footnote gives a $34.51 wage and a compensation factor based on $50.31 divided by $34.59.[@SRC-0176|Item 12, applicant wage footnote and CHRC/STA calculations]

**Classification and significance.** This is a rate/version ambiguity. The applied value and stated transformation conflict; the intended input is unresolved. Substituting the higher value would exceed the evidence.

**Direct control.** Retain the source wage, compensation ratio, transformation, and applied value together; generate the footnote from those inputs. Analysts must resolve conflicts.

### Surface Cybersecurity: workload and proxy alignment

**Published issue.** The Federal workload rows total **1,149 hours**, compared with **881 hours** reported. Separately, the contact-update estimate combines an average monthly separations proxy with annual residential mobility. Those inputs differ in period and event definition, and their overlap is unresolved.[@SRC-0022|Item 14, Table 8; Item 12, coordinator updates and source footnotes]

**Classification and significance.** The total is a displayed arithmetic discrepancy; the contact estimate is a source-transformation problem. A residential move need not change a professional contact. The package was pending at retrieval, not an approved final revision.

**Direct control.** Reconcile workload totals mechanically. Separately require compatible periods, explicit event definitions, and a defensible mapping from source population to modeled activity.

## What the pattern establishes

**Several published TSA Supporting Statements contain internal inconsistencies that stronger model-to-output controls could prevent.** These controls align calculations, tables, footnotes, and narrative. Separate representations can drift apart; this describes a design risk, not the undocumented causes of these cases. Internal model quality and any causal role of manual processing remain unestablished.

Possible pathway duplication requires a scope decision; small precision residuals require a rounding check. The technical report retains these distinct additional cases.

# 4. What TSA should do

## Prioritize controls that resolve the observed risks

These recommendations translate the findings into branch practice.[@TECH|Section 10; sections 13-15]

| Action | Priority for Economic Analysis |
| **KEEP** | Preserve task-level activities, applicant/employer distinctions, year-specific cohorts, occupation rationale, and workload-based Federal costing. |
| **IMPROVE** | Validate population partitions and additive tasks; check units, rate periods, transformations, and arithmetic; synchronize tables, footnotes, and narrative; record support for material assumptions. |
| **ADOPT** | Use Item 15 bridges from identified prior estimates, an obligation ledger across collections, burden observations with measurement scope, and explicit internal-work/purchased-service distinctions. |
| **INVESTIGATE** | Resolve purchased-service routing, overhead overlap, Federal system allocations, and proxy validity before imposing common defaults. |

## Make the review outcomes explicit

**Population and pathway review.** Distinguish unique respondents from repeated responses. State whether activities are alternatives or additional tasks. Exclusive pathway shares should reconcile; recurring activities should not inflate the count of unique people.

**Source and rate review.** Preserve the source period, population, unit, transformation, and occupation rationale. Review proxy fit and aging inputs explicitly.

**Arithmetic and output review.** Reconcile activity rows, subtotals, and summaries at retained precision. Generate published numbers and source footnotes from the accepted model. After edits, compare the final statement with the model again.

**Assumption and scope review.** Identify unsupported timing or allocation assumptions, distinguish missing information from zero, and separate fee recovery from gross Federal resources. Resolve material scope questions rather than embedding an unexplained convention.

Distinguish failed arithmetic identities from analytical warnings. The former require reconciliation; the latter require judgment and evidence.

# 5. The recommended analytical architecture

## One analytical source of truth

A common record should bind each material input to its source, unit, period, population, and transformation, and identify assumptions as assumptions. The resulting model should generate the Excel workbook and the Supporting Statement's quantitative content together.[@TECH|Sections 14-15]

**Figure 1. Recommended production approach.** Revisions return to the model before outputs are regenerated.

## What the system should check

Automate arithmetic, unit, period, population-partition, and Item 15 checks. Flag potential duplicate pathways and missing or stale sources. Generate numeric prose and footnotes from shared results, then check final exports for agreement.

Common totals address the cargo discrepancy; shared values address the aircraft conflict; source-bound rates address the airport conflict; workload and period checks address the cybersecurity findings.

## What analysts must decide

Population definitions, activity boundaries, task-time assumptions, occupation fit, proxy validity, cost scope, and classification remain human judgments. Software can flag an overlap or unsupported assumption; it cannot determine that the intended population or time is correct merely because an equation balances.

The expected benefit is less reconciliation and more focused review. Savings are unquantified and should be measured in the pilot. Detailed architecture is retained separately.

# 6. Recommended next steps

## A bounded starting point for discussion

**Near term: adopt common QA controls.** Assign responsibility for a shared review checklist covering populations, rates and periods, arithmetic, output agreement, assumptions, and Item 15. Apply it to an upcoming collection. Record unresolved questions separately from corrected arithmetic so review can focus on the decisions that remain.

**Next: prototype one complete analytical record.** Select a collection with distinct activities, respondent segments, Federal workload, and a usable prior baseline. Use the benchmark-derived model to generate a formula-preserving Excel workbook, tables, rate footnotes, and draft Items 12-15 narrative. Keep the pilot bounded to the selected collection before expanding its scope.[@TECH|Sections 10 and 14-16]

The pilot should demonstrate that a reviewer can trace a published value to its inputs and that changing an input updates every affected output. Require reconciled subtotals, explicit pathway relationships, consistent rate periods, and a quantified baseline bridge. Unsupported assumptions should remain visible for review rather than being replaced with convenient defaults.

**Then: compare assumptions across TSA collections.** Construct an evidence graph linking published sources, obligations, populations, activities, and assumptions. Begin with internal TSA consistency among similarly shaped collections: enrollment, recordkeeping, security-plan amendments, review tasks, and shared systems. Use other agencies secondarily to supply candidate methods and comparison questions.

Differences warrant review, not automatic correction. Preserve task scope, population, mode, period, and evidence quality before standardizing.

**Longer term: integrate creation and review.** Combine validated modeling, evidence retrieval, and documented analytical decisions into a comprehensive ICR creation and review process. Expansion should follow demonstrated output consistency and usability.

## Decide whether to scale on observed results

For the pilot, track analyst preparation and reconciliation time, reviewer questions, traceability of material inputs, and contradictions remaining at final review. Establish a comparison basis before claiming gains; these are proposed success measures.

**If leadership wants to proceed,** the next decision would be to designate an analytical lead, select a pilot collection, and authorize a bounded prototype with the recommended controls as acceptance conditions.
