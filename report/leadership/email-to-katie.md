# Email to Katie

**Subject:** Federal ICR Items 12–15 Benchmark — Findings and a Capacity Demonstration

Katie,

I wanted to send you the attached ICR Items 12–15 benchmark brief. I think the findings are useful on their own, but I also think the process that produced it is the clearest example yet of the kind of analytical capacity expansion I have been trying to describe.

I started this morning with a fairly open-ended question: **How does TSA’s approach to Items 12–15 compare with the strongest recent federal practice, and what should a better analytical model look like?**

Rather than manually reviewing a handful of examples, I designed a structured research process with explicit evidence standards, a reconstruction rubric, source and claim tracking, a stopping rule based on analytical saturation, and a requirement to challenge the emerging conclusions before accepting them.

The progression this morning was roughly:

- **~5:52 a.m. — question formulated**
- **8:41 a.m. — evidence base frozen after reviewing 73 recent federal ICRs across 38 agency/component labels, including 10 TSA collections**
- **9:37 a.m. — complete 49-page technical benchmark produced from the frozen evidence**
- **~10:20 a.m. — separate 11-page leadership brief produced after an editorial compression pass**
- **~10:45 a.m. — final institutional-tone, evidence, citation, and presentation review completed**

The research did more than collect examples. It reconstructed published calculations where possible, compared methods across agencies, actively tested whether TSA could fairly be characterized as uniquely best-in-class, identified specific published TSA consistency and reconstruction issues, separated confirmed arithmetic discrepancies from ambiguity or unsupported inference, and converted the findings into concrete recommendations for how we could improve Items 12–15 production.

The substantive conclusion is balanced: **TSA has several strong practices worth preserving, particularly in Item 12, but no single agency has the complete best-practice model. The federal benchmark also identified several published TSA statements where calculations, tables, footnotes, or narrative do not fully reconcile. Stronger model-to-output controls could prevent those types of inconsistencies.**

The recommended direction is therefore not simply “automate ICRs.” It is to create one source-bound quantitative record that ties material inputs to their sources and produces the Excel model, tables, footnotes, and Supporting Statement from the same validated analysis, while leaving substantive assumptions and judgment with the economist.

The attached brief is the leadership-facing version. The full technical report, 73-ICR benchmark, scoring, source material, calculation checks, and supporting analytical record are retained separately if you or Jim want to interrogate any of the findings.

The other reason I wanted you to see this today is that **this entire research project ran while I continued normal branch work**. While the federal benchmark was being developed, I was also working through the stakeholder-feedback revision and sent you the detailed direction for that analysis.

That is the part I have been struggling to articulate about AI-enabled capacity. This was not primarily “AI wrote a report faster.” It was closer to **one economist being able to direct multiple analytical workstreams in parallel, spending human attention on problem definition, methodology, judgment, review, and what to do with the results rather than performing every intermediate research step sequentially.**

I would normally think of a benchmark with this scope as a project in its own right. This morning, it became one workstream alongside the rest of the work.

I’d appreciate your read when you have a chance. I think the substantive findings are worth discussing with you and Jim, and I think the way the work was produced is worth discussing separately as a demonstration of what this operating model may make possible.

Luke
