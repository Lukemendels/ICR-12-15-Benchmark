NOTE TO REVIEWER

The Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) is amending its existing standards to better protect miners against occupational exposure to respirable crystalline silica, a significant health hazard, and to improve respiratory protection for miners from exposure to airborne contaminants. MSHA has determined that under the Agency’s existing standards, miners at metal and nonmetal mines and coal mines face a risk of material impairment of health or functional capacity from exposure to respirable crystalline silica. MSHA’s final rule is titled “Lowering Miners’ Exposure to Respirable Crystalline Silica and Improving Respiratory Protection” (RIN 1219-AB36). In the final rule, MSHA establishes a uniform permissible exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica at 50 micrograms per cubic meter of air (µg/m3) for a full-shift exposure, calculated as an 8-hour time-weighted average, for all miners. MSHA’s final rule also includes other requirements to protect miner health, such as exposure sampling, corrective actions to be taken when a miner’s exposure exceeds the permissible exposure limit, and medical surveillance for metal and nonmetal mines. The final rule replaces existing requirements for respiratory protection and incorporates by reference ASTM F3387-19 Standard Practice for Respiratory Protection. 

As part of the Paperwork Reduction Act process, MSHA is submitting an information collection request (ICR) under OMB control number 1219-0156 for OMB’s review regarding the collection of information requirements associated with the final rule. 

Additionally, several provisions in the final rule affect two currently approved ICRs under OMB control numbers 1219-0011 and 1219-0048. Changes to these existing ICRs are summarized in their respective supporting statements.

A.JUSTIFICATION

1.  Explain the circumstances that make the collection of information necessary.  Identify any legal or administrative requirements that necessitate the collection.  Attach a copy of the appropriate section of each statute and regulation mandating or authorizing the collection of information.

Section 103(h) of the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977, as amended (Mine Act), 30 U.S.C. 813(h), authorizes the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) to collect information necessary to carry out its duty in protecting the safety and health of miners. Further, section 101(a) of the Mine Act, 30 U.S.C. 811(a), authorizes the Secretary of Labor (Secretary) to develop, promulgate, and revise, as may be appropriate, improved mandatory health or safety standards for the protection of life and prevention of injuries in coal metal, and nonmetal mines.

Under the authority granted by the Mine Act, MSHA publishes a final rule titled “Lowering Miners’ Exposure to Respirable Crystalline Silica and Improving Respiratory Protection” (RIN 1219-AB36). The rule establishes standards for occupational exposure to respirable crystalline silica. The standards, located in a new part 60 of title 30 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), establish a permissible exposure limit (PEL) of 50 micrograms of respirable crystalline silica per cubic meter of air (50 μg/m3) for a full-shift exposure, calculated as an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA) in all mines. The standards also establish an action level (AL) of 25 micrograms per cubic meter of air (25 μg/m3).

The new part 60 contains information collection requirements. These requirements include exposure monitoring and corrective actions (sections 60.12 and 60.13), respiratory protection (section 60.14), and medical surveillance for metal and nonmetal miners (section 60.15). Section 60.16 lists all the recordkeeping requirements related to part 60. Table 1 to section 60.16(a) lists the records the mine operator must retain and the retention period of each record. Each recordkeeping requirement is described in more detail below.

Table 1 to  Section  60.16(a)—Recordkeeping Requirements
Record | Section references | Retention period
1. Evaluation records | Section  60.12(c) | At least 5 years from date of each evaluation.
2. Sampling records | Section  60.12(g) | At least 5 years from sample date.
3. Corrective actions records | Section  60.13(c) | At least 5 years from date of each corrective action.
4. Written determination records received from a PLHCP | Section  60.14(b) | Duration of miner’s employment plus 6 months.
5. Written medical opinion records received from a PLHCP or specialist | Section  60.15(f) | Duration of miner’s employment plus 6 months.

Section 60.12 Exposure monitoring 

The samples identified in section 60.12 include: samples by the compliance date or first-time samples (section 60.12(a)(1)), additional samples or second-time samples (section 60.12(a)(2)), above-action-level-samples (section 60.12(a)(3)), corrective actions samples (section 60.12(b)), and post-evaluation samples (section 60.12(d)). Section 60.12(g) requires mine operators to make a record for each sample made under section 60.12. The sampling record must consist of the sample date, the occupations sampled, and the concentrations of respirable crystalline silica and respirable dust. The mine operator must post the sampling records and the laboratory reports on the mine bulletin board, and by electronic means if applicable, for the next 31 days upon receipt under section 60.12(g). Section 60.16(a)(2) requires that sampling records made under section 60.12(g) must be retained for at least 5 years from the sample date.

Under section 60.12(c), periodic evaluations must be conducted at least every 6 months or whenever there is a change in: production; processes; installation and maintenance of engineering controls; installation or maintenance of equipment; administrative controls; or geological conditions; mine operators are then required to evaluate whether the change may reasonably be expected to result in new or increased respirable crystalline silica exposures. Mine operators are required to make a record of each periodic evaluation, including the evaluated change, the impact on respirable crystalline silica exposure, and the date of the evaluation under section 60.12(c)(1). In addition, the mine operator is required to post the evaluation record on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, for 31 days, upon receipt under section 60.12(c)(2). Section 60.16(a)(1) requires that evaluation records made under section 60.12(c) must be retained for at least 5 years from the date of each sampling, evaluation, or corrective action.

Section 60.13 Corrective actions 

Under section 60.13, if any sampling indicates that a miner’s exposure exceeds the PEL, mine operators must: make approved respirators available to affected miners before the start of the next work shift, ensure that affected miners wear respirators properly for the full shift or during the period of overexposure until miner exposures are at or below the PEL, and immediately take corrective action to lower the concentration of respirable crystalline silica to at or below the PEL. Under section 60.13(c), mine operators are also required to make a record of corrective actions. Mine operators also must immediately report the result of any operator samples above the PEL to the MSHA District Manager or to any other MSHA office designated by the District Manager. 

The corrective actions record must include the corrective actions taken, including any related respirator use by affected miners, and the dates of the corrective actions.  Section 60.16(a)(3) requires that corrective action records made under section 60.13(c) must be retained for at least 5 years from the date of each corrective action, and these records must be stored with the records of related corrective action sampling.

Section 60.14 Respiratory protection

Section 60.14(a) addresses metal and nonmetal (MNM) mines only. It requires MNM mine operators to use respiratory protection as a temporary measure when miners must work in concentrations of respirable crystalline silica above the PEL while: (1) Engineering control measures are being developed and implemented; or (2) It is necessary by the nature of work involved (for example, occasional entry into hazardous atmospheres to perform maintenance or investigation).

Section 60.14(b) requires all mine operators – both coal and MNM – to temporarily transfer a miner either to work in a separate area of the same mine or to an occupation at the same mine where respiratory protection is not required if the miner has a written determination from a physician or other licensed health care professional (PLHCP) that the miner is unable to wear a respirator. Section 60.16(a)(4) requires the written determination record received from the PLHCP under section 60.14(b) to be retained for the duration of a miner’s employment plus 6 months. 

Section 60.14(c)(2) requires mine operators to have a written respiratory program when respirators approved by National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) are used. The written program must be consistent with the requirements of ASTM F3387-19: program administration; written standard operating procedures (SOPs); medical evaluation; respirator selection; training; fit testing; maintenance, inspection, and storage. 

Section 60.15 Medical surveillance for metal and nonmetal mines

Section 60.15(d)(1) requires MNM mine operators to ensure that the results of medical examinations or tests are provided from the PLHCP or specialist within 30 days of the medical examination to the miner, and, at the request of the miner, to the miner’s designated physician or another designee identified by the miner. Section 60.15(d)(2) requires MNM mine operators to ensure that, within 30 days of the medical examination, the PLHCP or specialist provides the results of chest X-ray classifications to NIOSH, once NIOSH establishes a reporting system. The mine operators are required to obtain a written medical opinion from the PLHCP or specialist within 30 days of a miner’s medical examination. 

The written medical opinion must contain the date of the medical examination, a statement that the examination has met the requirements of the section, and any recommended limitations on the miner’s use of respirators under section 60.15(e). Section 60.15(f) requires MNM mine operators to retain a record of the written medical opinions according to section 60.16(a)(5), which requires retention for the duration of a miner’s employment plus 6 months. 

Section 60.16 Recordkeeping requirements

Section 60.16(b) requires that upon request from an authorized representative of the Secretary, from an authorized representative of miners, or from miners, mine operators shall promptly provide access to any record listed in this section.

2.  Indicate how, by whom, and for what purpose the information is to be used.  Except for a new collection, indicate the actual use the agency has made of the information received from the current collection.

The required records for respirable crystalline silica exposure monitoring and corrective actions assist mine operators, miners, miner representatives, and state and federal regulators in tracking miners’ occupational exposure to respirable crystalline silica, detecting potential overexposures, determining the adequacy of control measures used to meet MSHA’s respirable crystalline silica standards, and identifying possible actions taken to control such exposure. 

The sampling and evaluation information collected by the mine operator is vital to the effective administration of a mine’s control of respirable crystalline silica exposures and allows the operator and MSHA to assess the programs’ effectiveness. The information collection requirement will provide mine operators with robust information to enable them to understand a history of occupational exposures at the mines and to take appropriate actions to protect miners, such as implementing engineering and administrative controls. Evaluation and sampling records can identify overexposures due to changes in production, processes, controls, or geological conditions. The information helps mine operators develop, implement, and adjust controls and other measures that protect miners from overexposures.

MSHA uses the information to determine how operators comply with the rule’s exposure monitoring requirements, which operators fail to protect miners from excessive respirable crystalline silica concentrations, and which operators need to take appropriate measures to lower respirable crystalline silica levels in the mine atmosphere. The information enables MSHA to provide needed compliance assistance. Mine operators also must immediately report the result of any operator samples above the PEL to the MSHA District Manager or to any other MSHA office designated by the District Manager.

Mine operators are required to notify miners of sampling and periodic evaluation results by posting them on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means. The information is used to protect miners from exposure to excessive levels of respirable crystalline silica by determining the need for additional engineering controls, future sampling, and/or corrective actions. 

The required medical information updates miners of their health status and potential overexposures to silica. The information also helps miners identify early symptoms of respirable crystalline silica-related diseases so that they can take necessary steps to protect their health. The information provides miners and their representatives with information about exposure patterns over time to understand health hazards at their mines and to make informed decisions about their health care. From MSHA’s experience and observation, informed miners who are aware of occupational health hazards around them are more likely to follow safe work practices and to report these hazards to their operators or MSHA when necessary. When miners are aware of occupational health hazards and participate in the identification, remediation, and control of those hazards, the overall level of safety and health at the mine will be improved. 

After a medical examination, the PLHCP will provide miners with the results of medical examinations and, once NIOSH establishes a reporting system, the PLHCP will provide NIOSH with the results of chest X-ray classifications. Mine operators will also receive a written medical opinion concerning recommended limitations on the miner’s use of a respirator. This information can be invaluable to miners who have already been diagnosed with an illness or experienced negative health effects and help them to make decisions about their health and future employment.


3.  Describe whether, and to what extent, the collection of information involves the use of automated, electronic, mechanical, or other technological collection techniques or other forms of information technology, e.g., permitting electronic submission of responses, and the basis for the decision for adopting this means of collection.  Also describe any consideration of using information technology to reduce burden.

Section 60.16 does not require records to be maintained in a specific format. MSHA’s longstanding practice is to allow operators to keep records in hard copy and/or electronic copy as long as the records are safeguarded and easily accessible. No specific information technology has been identified that would generally reduce the recordkeeping burden.

Under section 60.12(c)(1) and (2), the mine operator must make a record of the periodic evaluation and post the record on the mine bulletin board and if applicable, by electronic means, for the next 31 days. Under section 60.12(g), for each sample the mine operator is required to make a record of the sampling information and post the laboratory report on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, for the next 31 days, upon receipt. Section 109(a), 30 U.S.C. 819(a), of the Mine Act requires a mine operator to maintain a bulletin board where information can be posted and shared with miners and their representatives. For portable operations and other operators who prefer to communicate electronically, the final rule permits electronic notification in addition to posting the record on the bulletin board.

MNM mine operators are required to ensure that, within 30 days of the medical examination, the PLHCP or specialist provides the results of chest X-ray classifications to NIOSH once NIOSH establishes a reporting system. MSHA has determined that digital X-rays can be easily transmitted electronically to B readers anywhere in the United States for interpretation. Reporting will be implemented via a simple online survey collecting de-identified information from providers of surveillance services that does not allow the identification of individual miners. Examples of data elements to be collected include chest X-ray classification findings and a few other data elements (e.g., state, 5-year age group, 5-year tenure group, mining commodity).

4.  Describe efforts to identify duplication.  Show specifically why any similar information already available cannot be used or modified for use for the purposes described in Item 2 above.

No duplication of the information requested exists. Records are unique to each mine.

5.  If the collection of information impacts small businesses or other small entities, describe any methods used to minimize burden.

The information collection provisions apply to all mine operations, both large and small. Congress intended that the Secretary enforce the law at all mining operations within the Agency’s jurisdiction regardless of size and that information collection and recordkeeping requirements be consistent with efficient and effective enforcement of the Mine Act. [S. Rep. No. 95-181, 28 (1977)]. Section 103(e) of the Mine Act directs the Secretary not to impose an unreasonable burden on small businesses when obtaining any information under the Mine Act. MSHA considered the burden on small mines when developing the collection. However, MSHA judges that the burden on small mines cannot be reduced without adversely affecting MSHA’s respirable crystalline silica control enforcement efforts. Hence, MSHA believes that these information collection requirements are imposed on all mining operations and do not have a greater impact on small businesses or other small entities.

6. Describe the consequence to Federal program or policy activities if the collection is not conducted or is conducted less frequently, as well as any technical or legal obstacles to reducing burden.

MSHA would not be able to monitor potential overexposure, assess program effectiveness, and enforce the respirable crystalline silica standard and protect miners if MSHA did not collect this information. Less frequent monitoring of the quality of the mine air that miners breathe would provide an inadequate indication of the dust conditions to which miners are normally exposed. This would increase the likelihood that conditions with excessive respirable crystalline silica concentrations would go undetected. As a result, miners could suffer from material impairments of health or functional capacity caused by exposure to respirable crystalline silica and other airborne contaminants.

On July 13, 2023, MSHA published the proposed rule titled “Lowering Miners' Exposure to Respirable Crystalline Silica and Improving Respiratory Protection” in the Federal Register (88 FR 44852). The publication of the proposed rule notified the public that the current information collection requirements under OMB control number 1219-0156 were being created in accordance with the Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995, and provided 60 days for the public to submit comments. 

In the proposal, MSHA sought comment on the utility of the recordkeeping requirements in this section. MSHA received comments on the proposed recordkeeping requirements, including from an industrial hygiene professional association and mining trade association, supporting the Agency’s proposed recordkeeping provisions. A MNM operator and mining trade association opposed the recordkeeping requirements, stating that the requirements were duplicative and should be more flexible. The public comments were about record retention periods, access to records maintained under section 60.16, and other topics. Below is a detailed discussion of the comments received on this section. 

A. Records retention periods

Section 60.16 identifies recordkeeping requirements for records created in part 60. The final rule requires mine operators to retain evaluation, sampling, and corrective actions records for at least 5 years. The final rule requires mine operators to retain written determination records and written medical opinion records for the duration of a miner’s employment plus 6 months. It also requires mine operators, upon request from an authorized representative of the Secretary, from an authorized representative of miners, or from miners, to promptly provide access to any record listed in section 60.16. 

MSHA received comments from labor unions, advocacy organizations, one MNM operator, and a federal elected official requesting an increase in the retention periods for required sampling records. Records that were to be retained by the mine operator for at least 2 years under this section include evaluation, sampling, and corrective actions records, as described in proposed sections 60.16(a)(1) to (3),

The United Steelworkers (USW) and American Federation of Labor and Congress of Industrial Organizations (AFL-CIO) stated that increased record retention is particularly important for MNM mines, which are typically surface mines and are inspected less frequently than underground coal mines. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) recommended that, for MNM miners, operators should be required to keep records specified under sections 60.16(a)(1) to (3) for 30 years and to provide those records to the miner on termination of employment; operators be required to transfer records to a successor employer; and when an employer ceases operations and there is no successor employer to receive the record, the employer notify affected employees of their rights of access to records at least 3 months prior to the cessation of the employer’s business. Badger Mining Corporation stated that the sampling and corrective actions records proposed to be retained for at least 2 years should be required to be preserved indefinitely. Appalachian Voices recommended that all records regarding sampling be retained for longer than the life of the mine operation. 

USW and AFL-CIO expressed concern that retaining records for 2 years would be insufficient to establish a pattern of exposure or provide other critical information such as the evaluation of corrective actions. Labor unions, advocacy organizations, a MNM operator, and an individual suggested that MSHA should align its recordkeeping requirements with the OSHA silica standard recordkeeping requirements (29 CFR 1910.1020). 

In response to comments requesting an increase in the record retention period, the final rule increases the record retention period for evaluation, sampling, and corrective actions records in sections 60.16(a)(1) to (3) to at least 5 years. Increasing to the 5-year record retention period for evaluation, sampling, and corrective actions records will help mine operators, miners, and MSHA better evaluate and monitor changes in exposures, understand health hazards, and ensure the implementation and maintenance of proper controls to protect miners from health hazards associated with respirable crystalline silica.

Under final sections 60.16(a)(1) and (2), evaluation and sampling records confirm that sampling results accurately represent current exposure conditions. The 5-year recordkeeping requirement for evaluation and sampling records will provide mine operators with robust information to enable them to understand a history of occupational exposures at the mines and to take appropriate actions to protect miners, such as implementing engineering and administrative controls. Evaluation and sampling records can identify overexposures due to changes in production, processes, controls, or geological conditions. These records help mine operators develop, implement, and adjust controls and other measures that protect miners from overexposures. In addition, these expanded records will provide miners and their representatives with information about exposure patterns over time to understand health hazards at their mines and to make informed decisions about their health care. As some commenters noted, this information can be invaluable to miners who have already been diagnosed with an illness or experienced negative health effects and help them to make decisions about their health and future employment. The 5-year records of evaluation and sampling will also enable MSHA staff in Technical Support and Educational Field and Small Mine Services to provide needed compliance assistance.

The 5-year recordkeeping requirement for corrective actions records in final section 60.16(a)(3) will help mine operators and MSHA enforcement staff determine if existing controls are effective, or if maintenance or additional controls are needed. In MSHA’s experience, the cumulative record provides MSHA and mine operators with information to identify trends in exposures and operational changes. Mine operators can use trend information to determine the effectiveness of controls over time, and to take proactive measures to prevent future overexposures, while miners and their representatives can use the trend information to determine health hazards and protection needs at their mines.

MSHA has determined that the 5-year retention period in sections 60.16(a)(1) to (3) balances the operator’s burden to maintain records and the need for this information to take appropriate action to protect miners’ health. The 5-year record retention is also consistent with MSHA’s record retention period for operator samples collected for diesel particulate matter in underground metal and nonmetal mines (section 57.5071(d)(2)) and other injury and illness reports required for all mines (section 50.40). From MSHA’s experience and observation, informed miners who are aware of occupational health hazards around them are more likely to follow safe work practices and to report these hazards to their operators or MSHA when necessary. When miners are aware of occupational health hazards and participate in the identification, remediation, and control of those hazards, the overall level of safety and health at the mine will be improved. In sum, informed miners are more likely and better able to play an active role in safety and health as the Mine Act envisions and better protect themselves and other miners.

MSHA notes that minor changes have been made to final sections 60.16(a)(1) to (3) to change the citation for the records addressed and to reflect changes discussed in sections 60.12 and 60.13. MSHA has similarly revised the citations in Table 1 to Paragraph (a)—Recordkeeping Requirements.  

Like the proposal, final sections 60.16(a)(4) and (5) require that the written determination by a PLHCP that a miner is unable to wear a respirator under section 60.14(b), as well as the medical surveillance records under section 60.15(f), be retained for the duration of the miner’s employment plus 6 months. MSHA received several comments regarding the retention period for medical surveillance records, with most commenters supporting a longer retention period. 

UMWA recommended that medical surveillance records be kept for 30 years and provided to the miner on termination of employment; that operators be required to transfer records to a successor employer; and that when an employer ceases operations and there is no successor employers to receive the record, the employer be required to notify affected employees of their rights of access to records at least 3 months prior to the cessation of the employer’s business. The Association of Occupational and Environmental Clinics, the American Public Health Association (APHA), and USW suggested that MSHA aligns its recordkeeping requirements for medical surveillance records with the OSHA silica standard recordkeeping requirements (29 CFR 1910.1020). These same commenters, as well as a black lung clinic and an individual, suggested that, given the latency periods associated with health effects from silica exposure, medical surveillance records are invaluable for miners who are diagnosed with silica-related health conditions.

In response to these comments, MSHA reiterates that mine operators do not have access to a miner’s medical information and therefore do not maintain a record of such information. Only the PLHCP’s written determination made under section 60.14(b) on whether a miner is able to wear a respirator must be provided to mine operators. Under the final rule, as in the proposal, the mine operator will retain the written determination record for the duration of miner employment plus 6 months. 

Under final section 60.15(d), medical examination results must be provided to the miner, at the request of the miner, to the miner’s designated physician or another designee identified by the miner, and to NIOSH, once NIOSH establishes a reporting system. MSHA is not regulating the retention of medical examination results since they are not provided to the mine operator. The medical surveillance information (the written medical opinion records) that the mine operator will retain under final section 60.15(a)(5) includes a record of the date of the medical examination, a statement that the examination has met the requirements of this section, and any recommended limitations on the miner’s use of respirators. MSHA believes that retaining these medical surveillance records for the duration of the miner’s employment plus 6 months is appropriate. The requirement to retain records for an additional 6 months beyond the miner’s employment gives a miner more time to request records if the miner is employed at another mine. For example, a miner who was determined to be medically unable to wear a respirator may need this record for a new mine operator. The final rule does not increase the retention period because, as described above, the written medical opinion that the operator receives contains only basic information compared to the medical examination records that are in the miner’s possession and control. 

The Nevada Mining Association asked for clarification on the medical surveillance recordkeeping requirements, remarking that the rule does not include provisions requiring tracking of miners’ exposure throughout their careers and noting that miners often change companies over the course of their careers. This commenter asked whether it would be assumed that a miner’s occupational illness stems from work with their current employer, even if all samples and medical surveillance show the miner was not exposed above the PEL during their current employment. 

MSHA reiterates that each miner’s medical examination results are provided to that miner, to the miner’s physician or other designee at the request of the miner, and to NIOSH, once NIOSH establishes a reporting system. NIOSH’s reporting system, once established, will provide public health information on rates of silica-related disease, tenure, and prevalence in the MNM industry. 

Miners will have access to all medical examination results obtained under this part and will be able to track any impacts of exposure. The purpose of the medical surveillance examination requirements is to enable help miners to seek help from medical professionals who can identify early symptoms of respirable crystalline silica-related diseases and inform them of their health status, so that they can take early and necessary steps to protect their health. 

Vanderbilt Minerals, LLC stated that medical records are required to be collected under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and that an additional requirement by MSHA would be duplicative and unnecessary. MSHA clarifies that the mine operator is not responsible for obtaining and preserving the miner’s medical examination results or records. Therefore, there is no duplication of collecting medical records.

B. Access to records maintained under section 60.16

Final section 60.16(b), like the proposal, requires mine operators to make records in this section available promptly upon request to miners, authorized representatives of miners, and authorized representatives of the Secretary of Labor. A federal elected official stated that MSHA should require sampling records and any other information required to be posted on the mine bulletin board to be submitted to miner representatives. This commenter also urged MSHA to require operators to provide cumulative exposure records to the miner upon request, similar to 30 CFR 57.5040. A miner health advocate suggested that corrective actions records should be required to be submitted to MSHA and miner representatives. 

After considering the comments, MSHA determined that no change to final section 60.16(b) is necessary. The requirement to provide all the listed records promptly upon request to miners, authorized representatives of miners, and authorized representatives of the Secretary of Labor ensures that miners and MSHA will have access to records as needed, which facilitates enforcement and transparency. Miners, miners’ representatives, and MSHA can request the records in this section at any time; therefore, MSHA has determined that it is not necessary to require operators to submit records to miners, miners’ representatives, and MSHA without request. 

C. Other comments

The APHA suggested that all required records should be made available to NIOSH. As discussed in response to comments under section 60.15(d)(2), MSHA is requiring that the results of chest X-ray classifications obtained under medical surveillance examinations be made available to NIOSH. MSHA has determined that it is not necessary to provide other records required under part 60 to NIOSH. 

The American Industrial Hygiene Association supported the proposed recordkeeping requirements and recommended that operators be required to develop and maintain exposure control plans that identify the tasks that involve miners’ exposure above the PEL and the methods used to protect miners, including procedures to restrict access to work areas where high exposures may occur.

After considering the comment, MSHA has concluded that an exposure control plan record is not necessary because of the sampling and control methods required. As required under part 60, mine operators must use engineering and administrative controls to prevent overexposures to respirable crystalline silica. Under section 60.12(c), mine operators are required to evaluate these controls at least every 6 months or whenever there is a change in production, processes, installation and maintenance of engineering controls, installation and maintenance of equipment, administrative controls, or geological conditions to determine if the change is reasonably expected to result in new or increased respirable crystalline silica exposures. The operator must make a record of the evaluation, including the evaluated change, the impact on respirable crystalline silica exposure, and the date of the evaluation and post the record on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, for the next 31 days. Operators are expected to conduct these evaluations to assess changing conditions on a regular basis to ensure miners are not exposed at levels above the PEL. The evaluation records provide important information to mine operators to enable them to implement effective control methods to protect miners, to identify occupations and work areas where there is a risk of overexposure, and to make necessary adjustments. MSHA has determined requiring exposure control plan records is not necessary.

This record will enable operators and miners to identify those tasks where overexposures may have occurred and individuals who may be overexposed, as the commenter suggested. Under section 60.13(b), operators must make a record of any corrective actions. This record will provide mine operators with necessary information to determine which control methods should be developed, implemented, and maintained to prevent exposures above the PEL. Miners can use this information to take a proactive approach to their health.

First-time and second-time sampling
According to section 60.12(a) of the rule, coal mine operators will need to perform first-time and second-time sampling within 360 days of the publication date of this rule to assess the full shift, 8-hour TWA exposure of respirable crystalline silica for each miner who is or may be expected to be exposed to respirable crystalline silica. MNM mine operators are required to do the same within 720 days of the publication date of this rule. The implementation period begins 360 days after the publication of this rule.

The respondents consist of all active mines because each operator of an active mine is required to perform first-time and second-time sampling. MSHA counts the number of active mines in 2019, defining an active mine as one that had at least 520 employment hours (equivalent to 1 person working full time for a quarter of a year) in at least one quarter of 2019. Using this definition based on 2019 employment data, MSHA estimates that 1,106 coal mines will generate first-time and second-time sampling records in the first year of implementation, year 1. Due to the year between the beginning of the compliance period for coal and MNM mines, MSHA estimates that 11,525 MNM mines will conduct these first-time and second-time samples in year 2.

MSHA assumes that a sample comprising at least 50 percent of miners at mines that employ 20 or fewer miners (including contract workers) will be necessary to collect a representative sample. In mines with 20 to 100 miners, a minimum 25 percent of miners will need to be sampled for the sample to be representative, while a minimum 15 percent of miners need to be sampled to ensure the samples are representative at mines employing at least 100 miners. MSHA assumes that on average 2 samples are taken from each of the selected miners.

Above-action-level Sampling

According to section 60.12(a)(3) of the rule, mines where the most recent sampling indicates that miner exposures are at or above the action level but at or below the PEL, the mine operator must continue to sample within 3 months of the previous sampling. Because above-action-level sampling will start after first-time and second-time sampling, it is expected to be carried out starting in the second half of year 1 for coal mines and the second half of year 2 for MNM mines.

MSHA estimates that the number of respondents required to make above-action-level sampling records will decrease each year. MSHA assumes that the shares vary by commodity and are expected to decrease linearly each year until reaching a steady state in year 7. For example, the share of coal samples at or above the action level is assumed to decrease from 18.7 percent in the second half of year 1 to 18.2 percent in year 2 and 17.5 percent in year 3. This leads to the sampling being carried out in 206 coal mines (=1,106 mines x 18.7 percent of samples over the action level) in year 1, 201 coal mines (=1,106 x 18.2 percent) in year 2 and 193 coal mines (=1,106 x 17.5 percent) in year 3. Similarly, MSHA estimates that for MNM, there will be 3,489 affected mines (=11,525 x 30.3 percent) in year 2 and 3,196 mines (=11,525 x 27.7 percent) in year 3.

The estimated number of above-action-level samples is calculated based on the following factors: 
The number of miners with sampling results at or above the new action level. As a result of greater compliance, MSHA assumes that the share of miners reporting above-action-level samples will linearly decrease each year until reaching a steady state in year 7. For MNM, the share of miners reporting exposure that are at or above the action level declines from 30.3 percent in the second half of year 2 to 27.7 percent in year 3. MSHA estimates that the number of miners who will need conduct above-action-level sampling is 66,222 in the second half of year 2, and 120,960 in year 3. 

For coal, the share of miners reporting exposure that are at or above the action level declines from 18.7 percent in the second half of year 1 to 18.2 percent in year 2 and 17.5 percent in year 3. MSHA estimates that the number of miners who will need conduct above-action-level samples is 13,727 miners in the second half of year 1, 26,719 miners in year 2, and 25,741 miners in year 3.

The percent of miners needed for representative samples. MSHA assumes representative samples will consist of samples from 50 percent of miners from mines with 20 or fewer miners, 25 percent from mines with between 20 and 100 miners, 15 percent from mines with more than 100 miners. The miner numbers include contract workers.

The sampling frequency based on the number of quarters per year that mines operate. MSHA assumes that mines will sample once per quarter, such that a mine that operates 4 quarters per year will conduct sampling 4 times per year, a mine that operates 3 quarters per year will conduct sampling 3 times per year, a mine that operates 2 quarters per year will conduct sampling 2 times per year, and a mine that operates 1 quarter a year will conduct sampling 1 time per year.

As a result, the number of the above-action-level samples in coal mines with 20 or fewer miners in the second half of year 1 will be:
 
Mines operating 4 quarters: 4,835 miners x 18.66 percent above action level x 50 percent representative sampling x 4 samples per year / 2 [due to half a year of sampling] = 902 samples.

Similarly, mines operating 3 quarters will collect 96 samples, mines operating 2 quarters will collect 40 samples, and mines operating 1 quarter will collect 12 samples. Total above-action-level samples is estimated to be 1,051 in the second half of year 1 in coal mines with 20 or fewer miners. Applying the same methodology to groups of other mine sizes, MSHA estimates that there will be 1,545 samples for coal mines with between 20 and 100 employees, and 2,827 samples from mines with more than 100 employees. Hence, there will be a total of 5,423 above-action-level coal samples in year 1. MSHA estimates that coal mines will conduct 10,556 above-action-level samples in year 2 and 10,170 samples in year 3.

Given that the compliance period for MNM mines will not start until a year after coal mines and given that MNM mines will not conduct above-action-level sampling until they first conduct first-time and second-time sampling, MSHA estimates that MNM mines will conduct 0 above-action-level samples in year 1, 37,719 samples in the second half of year 2, and 68,892 in year 3. Combining MNM and coal samples together, an annual average of 44,253 above-action-level samples will be conducted in the first three years, including 35,537 MNM samples and 8,716 coal samples. 

MSHA estimates that recordkeeping time for all types of samples at the mine is 15 minutes per sample, which includes the time needed to post the record and the laboratory report on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means. MSHA assumes that the recordkeeping time is valued at the average loaded hourly wage of an industrial hygienist making $76.74 per hour in MNM mines and $76.35 per hour in coal mines.

Corrective Action Sampling

According to section 60.12(b) of the rule, where the most recent sampling, including any sampling conducted by the operator, indicates that miner exposures are above the PEL, the mine operator must sample after corrective actions taken pursuant to section 60.13, until the sampling indicates that miner exposures are at or below the PEL. Because corrective actions sampling will start after first-time and second-time sampling, it is expected to be carried out only in the second half of year 1 for coal mines and the second half of year 2 for MNM mines.

Based on MSHA’s inspector sampling data (2005-2019 for MNM mines, and August 2016 to July 2019 for coal mines), 1,226 mines (1,096 MNM mines and 130 coal mines) were over the PEL in their most recent sampling event before the implementation of this rule. MSHA estimates that the number of respondents required to make corrective actions records will decrease each year based on the share of miners above the PEL. MSHA assumes that the shares vary by commodity and are expected to decrease linearly each year until reaching a steady state in year 7. For example, the share of coal samples above the PEL is assumed to decrease from 5.5 percent in the second half of year 1 to 5.0 percent in year 7. This leads to the corrective action sampling being carried out in 129 coal mines (=130 mines would have carried out corrective actions sampling before implementation x 5.48 percent of samples over the PEL in the second half of year 1 / 5.52 percent of samples over the PEL before implementation) in the second half of year 1, 127 mines (=130 x 5.41 percent / 5.52 percent) in year 2 and 125 mines (=130 x 5.33 percent / 5.52 percent) in year 3. Similarly, MSHA estimates that for MNM, the number of MNM mines expected to perform corrective actions sampling is 0 in year 1, 944 in year 2 and 868 in year 3.

The estimated number of corrective actions samples is calculated based on the following factors: 
The number of miners with sampling results above the PEL. As a result of greater compliance, MSHA assumes that the share of miners obtaining corrective actions samples will linearly decrease each year until reaching a steady state in year 7. For MNM, the share of miners reporting exposure that are above the PEL declines from 15.5 percent in the second half of year 2 to 14.2 percent in year 3. MSHA estimates that the number of MNM miners obtaining corrective actions samples also changes from 32,698 in the second half of year 2 to 60,129 in year 3. 

For coal, the share of miners reporting exposure that are above the PEL declines from 5.5 percent in the second half of year 1 to 5.4 percent in year 2 and 5.3 percent in year 3. MSHA estimates that the number of coal miners with sampling results above the PEL changes from 4,031 in the first half of year 1 to 7,966 in year 2, 7,838 in year 3. 

The percent of miners needed for representative samples. MSHA assumes representative samples will consist of samples from 50 percent of miners from mines with 20 or fewer miners, 25 percent from mines with between 20 and 100 miners, 15 percent from mines with more than 100 miners. The miner numbers include contract workers.

The sampling frequency based on the number of quarters mines will be in operation. MSHA assumes that mines will sample once per quarter, such that a mine that operates 4 quarters per year will conduct sampling 4 times per year, a mine that operates 3 quarters per year will conduct sampling 3 times per year, a mine that operates 2 quarters per year will conduct sampling 2 times per year, and a mine that operates 1 quarter a year will conduct sampling 1 time per year.

The number of corrective actions samples is increased by 25% to account for the case that some mines not correcting the problem on the first try. This assumes 80% of mines correct the problem on the first try, 15% of mines correct the problem on the second try, and 5% correct the problem on the third try.

As a result, the number of corrective actions samples in coal mines with 20 or fewer miners in the second half of year 1 is calculated as follows:
 
Mines operating 4 quarters: 4,835 miners x 5.5 percent above the PEL x 50 percent representative sampling x 4 samples per year x 1.25 additional corrective actions / 2 [due to half a year of sampling] = 331 samples per year.

Similarly, mines operating 3 quarters will collect 35 samples, mines operating 2 quarters will collect 15 samples, mines operating 1 quarter will collect 4 samples. The number of total corrective actions samples is estimated to be 386 in the second half of year 1 in coal mine with 20 or fewer miners. Applying the same methodology to groups of other mine sizes, MSHA estimates that there will be 567 samples for coal mines with employees between 20 and 100, and 1,038 samples from mines with more than 100 employees. Hence, there will be a total of 1,991 correction action samples in coal mines in year 1. MSHA estimates that coal mines will conduct 3,934 correction action samples in year 2 and 3,871 samples in year 3.

Given that the compliance period for MNM mines will not start until a year after coal mines and given that MNM mines will not conduct corrective actions sampling until they conduct first-time and second-time sampling, MSHA estimates that MNM mines will conduct 0 corrective actions samples in year 1, 23,414 in the second half of year 2, and 43,041 in year 3. Combining MNM and coal samples together, MSHA estimates that an annual average of 25,417 corrective actions samples will be conducted in the first three years, including 22,152 MNM samples and 3,265 coal samples. 

Each miner’s overexposure to respirable crystalline silica – which requires corrective actions sampling – will have to be followed by a report by the mine operator to the MSHA District Office or to any other MSHA office designated by the District Manager. 

The assumption for calculating corrective actions samples and post-evaluation samples was changed from the proposal. In the proposed rule, the number of corrective actions samples was combined with the number of post-evaluation samples and their sum was assumed to be equivalent to a constant 2.5 percent of all miners. In the final rule, the number of corrective actions samples is based on the projected share of samples over the PEL, while the number of post-evaluation samples alone is now 2.5 percent of miners per periodic evaluation. The change in methodology is intended to made estimates more consistent with existing sampling data.

MSHA estimates that recordkeeping time for all types of samples is 15 minutes per sample, which includes the time needed to post the record and the laboratory report on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, and is valued at the average loaded hourly wage of an industrial hygienist making $76.74 per hour in MNM mines and $76.35 per hour in coal mines. MSHA further estimates that the report to MSHA District Office or to any other MSHA office designated by the District Manager will take 5 minutes and be carried out by a clerk making $38.37 per hour in MNM mines and $41.83 per hour in coal mines.

Periodic Evaluations

According to section 60.12(c) of the rule, mine operators must carry out periodic evaluations at least every 6 months after commencing sampling under 60.12(a)(1) or whenever there is a change in production, processes, installation or maintenance of engineering controls; installation or maintenance of equipment; administrative controls; or geological conditions; mine operators shall evaluate whether the change may reasonably be expected to result in new or increased respirable crystalline silica exposures. Once the evaluation is completed, the mine operator must make a record of the evaluation and post the record on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, for the next 31 days.

MSHA assumes that all 12,631 mines will record periodic evaluation results. MSHA estimates that mines operating 3 or 4 quarters per year will conduct 2.4 periodic evaluations per year, and mines operating 1 or 2 quarters per year will conduct 1.2 periodic evaluations per year. MSHA increased the number of periodic evaluations by 20 percent from twice a year in the proposal because some mines, including portable mines, will need to perform evaluations more than twice per year due to changes in production, processes, installation or maintenance of engineering controls, installation or maintenance of equipment, administrative controls, or geological conditions as specified by the final rule.

For example, 935 coal mines are expected to operate 3 or 4 quarters per year in year 1. Those mines are expected to carry out 2,244 (=935 x 2.4) periodic evaluations in year 1. The other 171 coal mines are expected to operate 1 or 2 quarters per year in year 1. They are expected to carry out 205 (=171 x 1.2) periodic evaluations in year 1. Combined, 1,106 coal mines are expected to carry out 2,449 (=2,244 + 205) periodic evaluations in years 1, 2, and 3. 

Given that the compliance period for MNM mines will not start until a year after coal mines, MSHA estimates that MNM mines will carry out 0 periodic evaluations in year 1, and 25,859 in years 2 and 3.

MSHA estimates it will take an industrial hygienist, earning $76.74 per hour in MNM mines and $76.35 per hour in coal mines, 15 minutes to record an evaluation. Post-Evaluation Sampling

According to section 60.12(d) of the rule, if the mine operator determines as a result of the periodic evaluation that miners may be exposed to respirable crystalline silica at or above the action level, the mine operator must perform sampling to assess the full shift, 8-hour TWA exposure of respirable crystalline silica for each miner who is or may reasonably be at or above the action level.

The assumption for calculating corrective actions samples and post-evaluation samples was changed from the proposal. In the proposed rule, the number of corrective actions samples was combined with the number of post-evaluation samples and their sum was assumed to be equal to a constant 2.5 percent of all miners per periodic evaluation. In the final rule, the number of corrective actions samples is based on the projected share of samples over the PEL, while the number of post-evaluation samples alone is now equal to 2.5 percent of all miners per periodic evaluation. The change in methodology is intended to made estimates more consistent with existing sampling data.

MSHA expects mines operating 3 or 4 quarters per year to carry out 2.4 periodic evaluations per year, and mines operating 1 or 2 quarters per year to carry out 1.2 periodic evaluations per year. MSHA assumes that mine operators will need to carry out post-evaluation sampling for 2.5 percent of their miners after each periodic evaluation. For example, 72,741 miners, including contract workers, are expected to be employed in coal mines operating 3 or 4 quarters per year in year 1. Those miners are expected to obtain 4,364 post-evaluation samples in year 1 (=72,741 x 2.5 percent x 2.4 times). The other 835 coal miners are expected to be employed in coal mines operating 1 or 2 quarters per year in year 1. Those miners are expected to obtain 25 post-evaluation samples in year 1 (=835 x 2.5 percent x 1.2 times). Combined, coal miners are expected to obtain 4,390 (=4,364 + 25) post-evaluation samples in years 1, 2, and 3.


Given that the compliance period for MNM mines will not start until a year after coal mines, MSHA estimates that MNM mines will conduct 0 post-evaluation samples in year 1, and 12,564 in years 2 and 3.

The number of respondents is calculated as the number of post-evaluation samples in a given year x the portion of mines operating 3 or 4 quarters that year x 2.5 percent x 2 times + number of post-evaluation samples in a given year x the portion of mines operating 1 or 2 quarters that year x 2.5 percent x 1 time. For example, 4,390 post-evaluation samples are expected to be taken in coal mines in year 1. Out of those samples, 98.9 percent are expected to be taken in mines operating 3 or 4 quarters per year and 1.1 percent in mines operating 1 or 2 quarters per year. Thus, 218 coal mines (=4,390 samples x 98.9 percent x 2.5 percent x 2 + 3,658 x 1.1 percent x 2.5 percent x 1) are expected to carry out the 4,390 post-evaluation samples in years 1, 2, and 3. Similarly, MSHA estimates that 623 MNM mines will conduct post-evaluation samples in years 2 and 3.

MSHA estimates that recordkeeping time for all types of samples is 15 minutes per sample, which includes the time needed to post the record and the laboratory report on the mine bulletin board and, if applicable, by electronic means, and is valued at the average loaded hourly wage of an industrial hygienist making $76.74 per hour in MNM mines and $76.35 per hour in coal mines.

Corrective Actions

According to section 60.13 of the rule, any sampling performed by the operator or MSHA that indicates that a miner’s exposure exceeds the PEL requires the mine operator to make approved respirators available to affected miners before the start of the next work shift; ensure that affected miners wear respirators for the full shift or during the period of overexposure until miner exposures are at or below the PEL; and immediately take corrective actions to lower the concentration of respirable crystalline silica to at or below the PEL. Mine operators need to make a record of corrective actions and the dates of the corrective actions.

As explained in the corrective actions sampling section, there was one assumption change relating to corrective actions between the proposed rule and final rule. In the proposed rule, the number of corrective actions was based on the number of corrective actions samples, which were combined with the number of post-evaluation samples. Their sum was equal to a constant 2.5 percent of all miners per periodic evaluation. In the final rule, the number of corrective actions samples is based on the projected share of samples over the PEL. The number of corrective actions is now based on the share of samples over the PEL as well.

Only those mines with at least one miner exposure above the PEL are assumed to carry out the requirement, with each corrective action being followed by a corrective actions sampling. The number of respondents for corrective actions is equivalent to the numbers for corrective actions samples, as shown in Table 12-4, given that each corrective action must be followed by corrective actions sampling. Table 12-4 provides the estimated number of mines conducting corrective action sampling as 129 in year 1, 127 in year 2, 125 in year 3 for coal; 0 in year 1, 944 in year 2, and 868 in year 3 in for MNM. MSHA estimates that an annual average of 731 mines (604 MNM mines and 127 coal mines) will require corrective actions each year, starting in the second half of year 1 for coal mines and second half of year 2 for MNM mines. 

MSHA estimates that the number of corrective action sampling is: for MNM, 0 in year 1, 23,414 in year 2, and 43,041 in year 3; for coal, 1,991 in year 1, 3,934 in year 2, and 3,871 in year 3. The annual average of corrective actions records will 25,417 (22,152 in MNM mines and 3,265 in coal mines). 

Based on changes to MSHA’s methodology, there is no longer a separate recordkeeping burden relating to operators noting the use of respirators during a corrective action (which was estimated at an annual average of 5,685 records in the proposal). In the final rule, MSHA assumes that operators noting the use of respirators during a corrective action will be done as part of the corrective actions records.

A clerk, earning $38.37 per hour in MNM mines and $41.83 per hour in coal mines, will need 5 minutes to enter the results of the corrective action record.

Respiratory Protection (Records of Medical Evaluation and Fit Testing)

Under section 60.13(a)(2) of the final rule, all mine operators must ensure that miners affected by a respirable crystalline silica overexposure wear respirators properly for the full shift or during the period of overexposure until miner exposures are at or below the PEL. According to section 60.14(a), MNM mine operators must use respiratory protection as a temporary measure when miners must work in concentrations of respirable crystalline silica above the PEL while: (1) engineering control measures are being developed and implemented; or (2) it is necessary by the nature of work involved (for example, occasional entry into hazardous atmospheres to perform maintenance or investigation).

According to section 60.14(b) of the rule, mine operators must update the information provided to the PLHCP concerning each miner’s job description and personnel records (e.g., fit testing results). The information includes work area; type and weight of respirator; duration and frequency of respirator use; work activities and environmental conditions; hazards; and other PPE worn. This information can then be used by the PHLCP or specialist to determine whether a miner is unable to wear a respirator. Upon written determination by a PLHCP that an affected miner is unable to wear a respirator, the miner will need to be temporarily transferred either to work in a separate area of the same mine or to an occupation at the same mine where respiratory protection is not required. 

MSHA assumes that all mines carrying out corrective actions, plus an additional 10 percent of all MNM mines, will have miners wearing respirators (as per section 60.14(a)). MSHA further assumes that in each mine where respirators are worn, an average of one miner will have a medical evaluation establishing their inability to wear a respirator. As described in Table 12-4, in year 1, 129 coal mines (equivalent to the number of coal mines undergoing corrective actions) will have 129 miners with a medical evaluation establishing their inability to wear a respirator. The number of coal miners is expected to decrease to 127 in year 2 and 125 in year 3. For MNM mines, the compliance period doesn’t begin until year 2. In year 2, 2,097 MNM mines (=944 mines undergoing corrective actions + 1/10 of 11,525 MNM mines) will have 2,097 miners with a medical evaluation establishing their inability to wear a respirator. In year 3, the number is expected to decrease to 2,021. 

MSHA estimates that it will take an industrial hygienist, earning $76.74 per hour in MNM mines and $76.35 per hour in coal mines, 30 minutes to provide a miner’s job description to the PLHCP and to document the PLHCP’s results of medical evaluation and/or fit testing, and written respirator determination in the miner’s records.

Medical Surveillance for MNM Miners

The final rule provides for both mandatory and voluntary medical exams for MNM miners. According to section 60.15(e) of the rule, the mine operator must obtain a written medical opinion from the PLHCP or specialist within 30 days of the medical examination. The written opinion must contain only the following: (1) the date of the medical examination; (2) a statement that the examination has met the requirements of this section; and (3) any recommended limitations on the miner’s use of respirators. Section 60.15(f) of the rule requires the mine operator to maintain a record of the written medical opinions received from the PLHCP or specialist.

MSHA assumes that 50 percent (an average of the 25 percent and 75 percent scenarios discussed in the rule’s Final Regulatory Impact Analysis) of eligible MNM miners (existing MNM miners), including contract workers, will make use of the opportunity to receive a voluntary medical examination that is paid for by their mine operator. As a result, MSHA expects 88,112 existing miners (=44,056 if 25 percent of existing MNM miners participate in voluntary medical exams in year 2 + 132,167 if 75 percent of MNM miners participate)/2) to receive voluntary medical exams in year 2 of this rule. The compliance period for MNM mines doesn’t begin until year 2. Every medical exam will be followed by a written medical opinion record.

MSHA further estimates that 8,392 miners in a given year, including contract workers, will be new miners entering the industry who will undergo mandatory medical examinations. MSHA estimated that the turnover of MNM miners will be 8,392 miners per year (1/22 of the estimated total of 184,615 MNM workers, measured in terms of FTE, with an average number of 22 years on the job before leaving the mining industry). Just like with voluntary exams for existing miners, the compliance period for mandatory medical examinations doesn’t begin until year 2. Every medical exam will be followed by a written medical opinion record.

As per section 60.15(d)(1), each operator of an MNM mine will need to provide to each miner periodic medical examinations performed by a PLHCP or specialist at no cost to the miner. Each mine operator will also need to provide the opportunity to have a medical examination at least every 5 years to all miners employed at the mine. The voluntary medical examinations will need to be available during an initial 12-month period, and at least every 5 years after the initial period during a 6-month period that begins no less than 3.5 years and not more than 4.5 years from the end of the last 6-month period. For each miner beginning working in the mining industry for the first time, the mine operator shall provide medical examinations during the initial 60 days after beginning employment, a follow up medical examination no later than 3 years after the initial examination, and a follow up medical examination no later than 2 years after the 3-year follow-up if the chest X-ray shows evidence of pneumoconiosis or the spirometry examination indicates of decreased lung function.

MNM mine operators must ensure that the results of medical examinations or tests be provided from the PLHCP to the miner within 30 days of the examination. The results of medical examinations will need to be provided only to the miner, and at the request of the miner, to the miner’s designated physician or another designee identified by the miner. MSHA estimates that this would result in a cost of $10 per miner ($10.71 in 2022 dollars) for the PLHCP or specialist to send a medical examination result to the miner. This cost is included in the physical examination cost element, part of the medical examination cost that will be paid by the mine operator to the PLHCP or specialist for medical examinations. (Medical examination entails the following cost elements: the physical examination, chest X-ray, spirometry test, lost work time while being examined, lost travel time, symptom assessment and occupational history, transportation cost, and recordkeeping of the mine operator). 

In a new requirement not presented in the proposed rule, section 60.15(d)(2) requires mine operators to ensure that, within 30 days of a medical examination, the miner’s PLHCP or specialist provides the results of chest X-ray classifications to NIOSH, once NIOSH establishes a reporting system. MSHA estimates that this would result in a cost of $10 per miner ($10.71 in 2022 dollars) for the PLHCP or specialist to send results of X-ray classification to NIOSH. This cost is included in the chest X-Ray cost element, part of the medical examination cost that will be paid by the mine operator to the PLHCP or specialist for medical examinations. (Medical examination includes a cost element of chest X-ray).
